About

Mia Pique is a Ph.D. student in Earth and Planetary Sciences at Stanford University's Doerr School of Sustainability, working in Professor Tiziana Vanorio's Rock Physics and Geomechanics Lab. Her doctoral research maps rare earth element deposits in carbonatite and alkaline igneous systems, combining rock physics with imaging techniques to better understand where critical minerals form and how to find them.

For her M.S., Mia studied how carbon byproducts from methane pyrolysis can strengthen white Portland cement, turning a waste stream into a performance-enhancing material for lower-carbon construction.

Before Stanford, Mia worked as a senior staff geologist in the Bay Area, directing geotechnical investigations for public clients like SFO, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, and the SFPUC, and conducted mineralogy, volcanology, and geochemistry research in Ecuador, Argentina, Australia, and the United States. A bilingual, first-generation Cuban-American scientist, she serves as an Access, Belonging, and Community Liaison for Stanford's Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences and is passionate about mentoring students and building inclusive STEM communities.

Research

Ph.D. · Critical minerals

Mapping rare earth elements in carbonatite and alkaline igneous systems

Rare earth elements underpin magnets, batteries, and much of the energy transition, yet supply is concentrated in a few places. My doctoral work characterizes where and how these elements occur in primary deposits, how they behave within mineral structures, and how rock physics and imaging can help locate and characterize them responsibly.

Rock physics · imaging · ArcGIS Pro · QGIS · Python

M.S. · Low-carbon geomaterials

Carbon byproducts from methane pyrolysis in white Portland cement

Methane pyrolysis produces hydrogen and a solid, carbon nanotube-bearing byproduct. I investigated incorporating this bed carbon into white Portland cement through additive-free mechanical processing. At 1 wt.% cement replacement, bed-carbon formulations reached roughly 21–23% higher compressive strength than the control.

P- and S-wave velocity · porosity · permeability · compressive and tensile strength · SEM/EDS
